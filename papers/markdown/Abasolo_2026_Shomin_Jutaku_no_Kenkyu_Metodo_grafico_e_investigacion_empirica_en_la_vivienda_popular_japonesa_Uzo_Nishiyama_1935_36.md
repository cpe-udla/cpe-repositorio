DEZAM and Alexander Klein’s
Graphic Method
In Kyoto, Nishiyama founded, together with
Shizutaro Ura, the independent collective
DEZAM. This collective brought together
research, graphic production and theoretical
debate in dialogue with the European avantgardes and the CIAM (Nakabayashi, 2018),
and published seven issues of its eponymous
journal between 1930 and 1932.
In the first issue of the journal, Nishiyama
contributed Manga shōsetsu: Kōtō gakkō jidai
(Graphic novel: High school years), in which
he narrated, exclusively through images, his
experiences from the last year of secondary
school to his first year at university. This
work shows his early capacity to represent
space from a narrative and visual perspective,
anticipating the centrality that his graphic
methods would acquire as tools of architectural
analysis. (Fig. 02-06).
In issue 5 of the journal DEZAM, published
in 1932, Nishiyama contributed two articles
that show his first theoretical approaches
to the architecture of the European Modern
Movement. The first analyses the architecture for
the great city by Ludwig Hilberseimer, presented
in his book Groszstadt Architektur (1927). The
second, entitled Kenchiku keikaku no keitōka no
kōsatsu (Considerations on the systematisation
of architectural planning), examines the graphic
analyses in plan developed by Alexander Klein
(1879–1960) and proposes a critical reflection
on them (Nishiyama, 2000, p. 12) (Fig. 7).
While the first text reveals an early interest in
urban planning and the functional organisation
of metropolitan space, the second highlights
the influence exerted by Klein’s work on
Nishiyama’s architectural thought, especially
in relation to minimum dwelling. This method,
developed in Europe as a response to the
housing crisis following the First World War,
was oriented towards the design of modern
and efficient popular dwellings, through the
optimisation of floor plans without the need to
increase their surface area.
The core of his proposal was a “scientific
procedure of a graphic type for the comparison
and assessment of dwelling plans” (Klein, 1980,

Shomin jūtaku no kenkyū
In 1935, Nishiyama initiated the research
programme Shomin jūtaku no kenkyū, whose
central instrument was the sumikkata chōsa
(survey on the way of dwelling), an empirical
recording method based on individual record
cards, architectural sketches and on-site
observation (Nakabayashi, 2018). An initial
proposal for the study of dwellings in Osaka
was submitted to the AIJ, but it was rejected
on the grounds that a sample of one hundred
dwellings was not statistically significant
to produce conclusive results (Nishiyama et
al., 2017). Far from abandoning his initiative,
Nishiyama reformulated and significantly
expanded the scope of the study, proposing
the analysis of three thousand dwellings
belonging to middle and popular sectors in
three cities: Osaka, Kyoto and Nagoya. The
research, entitled Shomin jūtaku no kenkyū
(Study on popular housing), would become the
first systematic and exhaustive investigation
on urban housing in Japan (Nakabayashi,
2018), in a context in which housing studies at
a national scale did not yet exist.

Machiya
Among the typologies of popular housing
addressed by Nishiyama, the machiya and
the nagaya stand out, both representative of
the traditional Japanese urban landscape.
This article focuses on the machiya, a
traditional Japanese urban typology whose
very name accounts for its hybrid condition.
Etymologically, the term is composed of two
kanji characters: machi (町), meaning “city”
or “town”, and ya (家 or 屋), corresponding
to “house” or “shop”, depending on the
variant employed. In this way, the machiya
refers both to its insertion in the urban fabric
and to its domestic and, in many cases,
commercial character. These are terraced
houses built on long and narrow plots, with
a narrow façade oriented towards the street,
especially characteristic of Kyoto and Osaka.
This configuration responded in part to the tax
system of medieval Japan, which calculated
taxes according to the width of the façade,

Machiya
Entre las tipologías de vivienda popular abordadas por Nishiyama destacan
la machiya y la nagaya, ambas representativas del paisaje urbano tradicional
japonés. Este artículo se centra en la machiya, tipología urbana tradicional
japonesa cuyo propio nombre da cuenta de su condición híbrida. Etimológicamente, el término se compone de dos caracteres kanji: machi (町), que
significa “ciudad” o “villa”, y ya (家 o 屋), que corresponde a “casa” o
“tienda”, según la variante empleada. De este modo, la machiya remite tanto
a su inserción en el tejido urbano como a su carácter doméstico y, en muchos
casos, comercial. Se trata de casas adosadas construidas en terrenos largos
y angostos, con fachada estrecha orientada a la calle, especialmente características de Kioto y Osaka. Esta configuración respondía en parte al sistema
tributario del Japón medieval, que calculaba los impuestos según el ancho de
fachada, lo que incentivó frentes angostos independientemente de la superficie total del terreno (Knoroz, 2022).
Nishiyama documentó esta evolución incremental a través de una serie de
bocetos axonométricos, en los cuales centró su atención en el área conocida

Notas finales sobre el dibujo como instrumento de
investigación
El trabajo de campo de Shomin jūtaku no kenkyū se realizó entre 1935 y
1936, pero el programa continuó desarrollándose durante el período bélico.
La interrupción forzada por el servicio militar —Nishiyama fue llamado a
filas en 1937 y dado de baja en 1940— no detuvo la investigación; a su regreso publicó una serie de artículos en el boletín del Architectural Institute of
Japan con avances del estudio, y en 1943 recibió el Premio Académico de esa
institución por sus estudios sobre vivienda popular (Nakabayashi, 2018). En
1947, ya como docente de la Universidad de Kioto, obtuvo su doctorado en
ingeniería con la tesis Shomin jūtaku no kenkyū, consolidando formalmente
un programa empírico iniciado más de una década antes.
Los resultados que arrojó su investigación sustentaron directamente su
teoría Shokushin Bunrin Ron (Teoría de la separación entre comer y dormir)
(Nishiyama, 1942). A partir del análisis de cientos de viviendas, Nishiyama
propuso que debía garantizarse una separación física entre dormitorios y

Nishiyama’s methods offer to the current
researcher who uses graphic resources at
least three lessons: the epistemic autonomy
of the graphic record —drawing not as the
representation of an already known object
but as a process of construction of knowledge
about inhabited space—; the construction
of one’s own notation systems that translate
conditions of habitability into comparable
visual information; and the integration of
fieldwork into the graphic process, where
record cards and drawing operate as a single
simultaneous analytical act. This sensibility
runs through contemporary Japanese

Bow-Wow, whose systems of representation

systematic method of fieldwork —which

logic of Nishiyama’s record cards; and finally
in the graphic work of Junya Ishigami, where
drawing —of extreme technical precision and

relevance of architectural representation for
producing knowledge before the building exists.
