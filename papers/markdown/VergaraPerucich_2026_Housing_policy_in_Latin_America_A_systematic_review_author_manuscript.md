Housing policy in Latin America: A systematic review

Francisco Vergara-Perucich1

Chile. jvergara@udla.cl

Núcleo de Investigación Centro Producción del Espacio, Universidad de Las Américas, Santiago,

This systematic review explores the academic production on housing policy in Latin

America between 2014 and 2024, analysing 1,781 peer-reviewed articles indexed in

Scopus and Web of Science. Using a two-stage bibliometric method, the study first

maps thematic trends across the region, then delves into three interrelated domains:

the financialisation of housing, socio-spatial inequality, and the climate crisis.

Findings reveal an increasingly consolidated field wherein financial logics

reconfigure housing provision, inequalities are deepened through market-led reforms,

and environmental imperatives reframe housing as a site of energy governance.

Rather than isolated phenomena, these axes are tightly interwoven, suggesting the

emergence of a systemic governance challenge. The study critiques the persistence of

technocratic and market-centric approaches in policy design, arguing that a rights-

based and context-sensitive agenda is urgently needed. It also underscores limitations

related to language bias and the underrepresentation of grey literature. The paper

advocates for future research that engages critically with the ontological foundations

of housing policy and embraces interdisciplinary, multilingual, and socially grounded

perspectives.

Keywords: housing policy; Latin America; financialisation; inequality; sustainability; bibliometric
review

In the pursuit of social and economic stability, secure access to housing has become a critical global

concern, one increasingly threatened by structural and financial pressures (Agha et al., 2024). In

Latin America, housing policy has long served as a tool of state intervention, aiming to steer market

dynamics towards more inclusive, environmentally responsible, and socially equitable housing

models. The quality, safety, and affordability of housing remain decisive for human well-being and

public health (Baker & Bentley, 2023; Clapham et al., 2017). Yet the housing market also represents

a core source of profit extraction, exposing this vital infrastructure to the volatility of capital

circulation (Aalbers & Christophers, 2014). Housing thus sits at a fraught intersection: essential to

vulnerable households seeking upward mobility, but simultaneously priced out by speculative

demand. In navigating these tensions, states are compelled to formulate housing policy with great

precision, balancing intervention with market discipline (Camargo Sierra, 2020; Carrasco-Gallego,

2020; Duca et al., 2021; Zavisca & Gerber, 2016; Fowler & Farrell, 2017).

1. Introduction

A substantial body of literature details the persistent challenges of housing policy across the region.

These include entrenched socio-spatial inequalities (Deneulin & Sánchez-Ancochea, 2018), limited

infrastructure to address informal settlements (Morales Martínez, 2017; Matus Madrid et al., 2019),

forced displacement driven by real estate-led development (Alova & Burgess, 2016; Angotti &

Irazábal, 2017; Monkkonen, 2018; Cociña, 2021), and the privatisation of public assets as a

generator of new inequalities (Molina et al., 2019; Arundel, 2017). Climate change and, more

recently, the public health crisis sparked by the COVID-19 pandemic have also reconfigured

housing needs (Walteros & Ramírez, 2020; Valderrama-Ulloa et al., 2023; Flynn, 2022). These

pressures have, in turn, spurred innovation in public policy—particularly in governance

frameworks, the management of public resources, sustainable urban planning, and infrastructure

development (Klaufus & Jaffe, 2015; Sanfelici, 2016; Morales Martínez, 2017). Analysing how

these policy priorities have evolved in the academic literature offers insight into shifting agendas,

emerging concerns, and persistent gaps.

This article conducts a bibliometric review of housing policy literature in Latin America to identify

the main research frontiers that developed between 2014 and 2024. Following an initial mapping of

the field, three priority themes emerge: housing financialisation, socio-spatial inequality, and the

climate crisis. Each of these domains is then examined through advanced bibliometric techniques to

generate a structured overview of critical housing policy debates. The findings are intended to guide

### Page 3

policymakers in identifying where renewed attention and intervention are most urgently needed to

shape future policy instruments.

2. Methodology

This study conducts an advanced bibliometric analysis to investigate the scientific production

landscape on housing policy in Latin America between 2014 and 2024. It aims to identify research

trends, thematic priorities, niche areas, interconnections, and knowledge gaps within the scholarly

literature. The study unfolds in two stages: (1) a broad bibliometric scan to detect the region’s key

emerging topics, and (2) a focused analysis of the most prominent thematic domains.

The dataset was compiled using indexed documents from Web of Science and Scopus, applying the

following search parameters:

For Web of Science (advanced search):
ts=(argentina OR bolivia OR brazil OR chile OR colombia OR "costa rica" OR "el
salvador" OR honduras OR nicaragua OR guatemala OR mexico OR ecuador OR peru OR
uruguay OR venezuela OR paraguay OR jamaica OR panama OR "dominican republic" OR
haiti OR cuba) AND ts=housing AND ts=(policy OR policies). Publication years: 2014–
2024.

For Scopus (advanced search):
TITLE-ABS-KEY(argentina OR bolivia OR brazil OR chile OR colombia OR "costa rica"
OR "el salvador" OR honduras OR nicaragua OR guatemala OR mexico OR ecuador OR
peru OR uruguay OR venezuela OR paraguay OR jamaica OR panama OR "dominican
republic" OR haiti OR cuba) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY(housing) AND TITLE-ABSKEY(policy OR policies) AND PUBYEAR > 2013 AND PUBYEAR < 2025.

Following data collection, an ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) process was undertaken to refine and

consolidate the datasets before analysis in R, using the bibliometrix package (Aria, 2017), which

supports comprehensive and efficient bibliometric processing. The final dataset comprised 1,781

documents from 923 different sources, with an average annual growth rate of 2.39% and a mean

citation rate of 7.95 per document. From the initial analysis, three key domains emerged for in-

depth exploration:

(1) Financialisation and Housing Policy in Latin America
(2) Inequality (including poverty and informality) and Housing Policy in Latin America
(3) Climate Crisis (including sustainability and energy)

### Page 4

Each domain was analysed through a segmented approach focused on: (i) most frequently used

author keywords, (ii) top-cited documents within each subset, (iii) keyword trends across three time

periods, and (iv) thematic categorisation of keywords into niche, emerging, basic, and motor

themes.

This study has several limitations. First, bibliometric methods—while effective for mapping trends

and identifying clusters—do not enable in-depth qualitative interpretation of regional specificities,

as abstracts rarely capture the full scope of article content. Second, although the study maps

academic production on housing in Latin America, it does not engage in cross-country policy

comparisons; the region’s institutional and socio-environmental heterogeneity renders such

comparisons methodologically fraught. Third, and most significantly, the reliance on Scopus and

Web of Science introduces a notable linguistic and regional bias1. The sample is predominantly

composed of English-language publications, with a significant underrepresentation of research

published in Spanish and a complete omission of articles in Portuguese. This is a major limitation,

as it excludes the vast body of knowledge produced in leading Latin American journals and regional

databases such as SciELO and Redalyc, particularly from key countries like Brazil, which figures

prominently in our thematic analysis. While this study acknowledges this bias, future research must

actively work to correct it. The focus, therefore, lies on identifying recurrent conceptual frameworks

and prominent themes rather than producing generalisable conclusions.

It is also acknowledged that aggregated trends may obscure locally grounded contributions shaped

by specific positionalities. Rather than addressing this as a methodological flaw, the study proposes

that the insights presented here serve as a foundation for future contextual and comparative

research—particularly those employing thematic analysis or grounded theory to interrogate

dominant paradigms and research blind spots in greater depth. It is important to note that this

systematic keyword-based search, while ensuring replicability, may retrieve highly cited global

documents whose primary focus is not Latin America or housing policy but are included due to

matching terms in their title, abstract, or keywords. Their inclusion, particularly in citation analyses,

reflects their influence within the specific thematic field constructed by the search query.

3. Results
This section presents the findings of the comprehensive bibliometric analysis of Latin American
housing policy research published between 2014 and 2024, with the aim of mapping its intellectual
structure and thematic trajectory. The section’s rationale is to move from a wide-angle, panoramic
view of the field to a focused, high-resolution analysis of its most critical research frontiers. To
achieve this, the analysis is structured in two distinct stages. The first stage provides a broad

### Page 5

cartography of the research landscape, identifying four principal thematic axes—disaster risk
management, socio-economic inequality, sustainability, and governance—and tracking their
evolution over time. This initial mapping reveals a shift in scholarly focus, moving away from
foundational public health and methodological inquiries towards a more applied and critical
engagement with public policy and urban governance.

This broad overview provides the justification for the second stage, which delves into the three
priority domains that emerged as most dynamic and influential. These focused sections explore (1)
the financialisation of housing, examining its consolidation as a core analytical framework; (2) the
persistent challenge of inequality, now critically viewed through the lens of neoliberalism; and (3)
the growing energy imperative, driven by the climate crisis and centred on sustainability and
resilience. The main findings present a marked maturation of the field. Evidence show that research
on Latin American housing is no longer disparate but is converging around a critical new nexus.
The once-separate streams of finance, social justice, and environmental sustainability are now
understood as deeply interconnected forces that are actively reshaping housing systems across the
region, thereby setting a clear and urgent agenda for future scholarship.

3.2. Rationale for Emerging Cluster Identification

The bibliometric analysis reveals five principal thematic clusters within Latin American housing

policy research (2014–2024): urban economics, inequality, sustainability, public health, and gender.

Within the Urban Economics cluster, the financialisation of housing stands out as a critical and

expanding research frontier. Here, housing is reframed not as a basic right but as a financial asset

(Nascimento Neto & Salinas Arreortúa, 2020). Recent studies document how certain state-led

policies have facilitated the accumulation of private capital (Molina et al., 2019; Zapata, 2018),

often triggering state-driven gentrification and urban displacement (López-Morales et al., 2021).

Financial actors such as real estate investment trusts (REITs) (Sanfelici & Halbert, 2019) and the

growing use of housing microfinance (Grubbauer, 2019) reinforce this shift. A consistent emphasis

on privatisation and its spatial consequences (Offner, 2019; Holland & Schneider, 2017; Monras,

2020) positions financialisation as a key site of inquiry in Latin American housing studies.

The Inequality cluster reflects the persistence of socio-economic disparities. Urban informality

persists as both a structural challenge and a survival strategy, despite poverty alleviation efforts

### Page 9

(Abufhele Milad, 2023; Sheppard et al., 2020). Recent research explores the intersection between

housing and informal labour markets (García et al., 2024), the limitations of public policy in

addressing vulnerability (Molina et al., 2019; Monkkonen, 2018), and the historical evolution of

informal settlements (van Gelder et al., 2016; Soares Gonçalves, 2015). These inequalities are also

linked to vulnerability during health crises (Rocha et al., 2021) and energy poverty (Reyes et al.,

2019), underscoring the multidimensional nature of exclusion in the housing sphere.

Sustainability has gained considerable prominence amid the global environmental crisis. Key

themes include energy poverty, climate vulnerability, and substandard housing (Mohsin et al., 2022;

Stojilovska et al., 2023). Strategies such as deep energy retrofitting (Tozer et al., 2023) and the

environmental potential of informal settlements (Reyes, 2021) are analysed in tandem with spatial

segregation and energy precarity (Encinas et al., 2022). This cluster also brings together work on

community resilience to environmental hazards (Cutter et al., 2014), sustainable development

governance (Ferro de Guimaraes et al., 2020), and sustainable energy consumption (Rao et al.,

2019), confirming the centrality of climate concerns in housing policy research.

Two further clusters, while not explored in depth in the next stage, remain relevant to recent

developments. The Public Health cluster gained momentum during the COVID-19 pandemic,

exposing how the crisis exacerbated pre-existing housing-related inequalities (Martínez-Valle,

2021; Benítez et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2020; Knaul et al., 2022; Gurney, 2021; Lima, 2023). The

Gender cluster investigates how housing inequality disproportionately affects women—especially

single mothers and low-income households—who are particularly vulnerable to structural forms of

urban violence (Lévy et al., 2017; Ludermir, 2023; Di Virgilio, 2017; Freire Santoro, 2019;

Vásquez-Vera et al., 2022; Moser, 2017).

3.3. Stage 2: In-Depth Analysis of the Three Thematic Axes

Following the general mapping, the second stage of the analysis delves into the three priority

domains identified: financialisation, inequality, and the climate crisis. This focused examination

uses advanced bibliometric techniques to explore the internal structure, conceptual evolution, and

key debates within each research frontier.

3.3.1. First Focus: Frontiers of Financialisation

The emergence of financialisation as a distinct thematic focus in recent years reflects a growing

conceptual recognition of its tangible impacts—impacts that are increasingly drawing the attention

### Page 10

of housing scholars. Initially framed as an institutional critique, the literature has progressively

shifted towards a situated understanding of the material consequences of financialisation in housing.

What this shift highlights is the once-overlooked presence of finance as a structuring force within

the design and delivery of housing. Traditional studies of housing finance have often missed how

these financial processes actively extract value from the very housing shortage they claim to address

—thereby reproducing the problem in a self-perpetuating loop. Table 2 lists the most influential

works in the sample, based on local citations.

The analysis identifies key publications that have shaped the discourse on housing financialisation

in Latin America between 2014 and 2024. Soederberg’s (2015) study of subprime mortgage

instruments in Mexico and Klink & Denaldi’s (2014) historical-geographical reading of Brazil’s

'My House, My Life' programme stand out, with 18 and 15 citations respectively. Reyes’ (2020)

examination of financialisation and housing access in Mexico also features prominently. Together,

these studies—largely focused on Mexico, Brazil, and Chile—unpack mechanisms of

financialisation (mortgage systems, public–private policy frameworks, and the role of finance-state

linkages), while also exposing the tensions between market dynamics and housing accessibility.

These dynamics are often embedded within broader neoliberal agendas that result in spatial

outcomes such as urban peripheralisation, particularly evident in Santiago and Mexico City.

3.3.2. Second Focus: Inequality under Neoliberalism

The persistent concern with inequality in Latin America reveals itself as a near-structural fixation

within housing policy research. The literature consistently reflects an epistemic imperative to

interrogate how inequality is spatially inscribed and socially contested. The symbiotic relationship

between neoliberal rationality and housing regimes—shaped through policy frameworks designed

to fit that rationality—illustrates how housing becomes a vehicle for reproducing socio-economic

hierarchies (Vergara-Perucich, J. F., & Aguirre-Nuñez, C., 2019; Garretón, 2017). This dynamic is

not merely a residual concern but a pathological condition embedded across almost every cluster

identified (Friendly, 2021). Each new crisis compounds this pattern, exposing novel expressions of

unequal access to housing. Such patterns are not accidental but the direct result of neoliberal

legacies—either through their continued implementation or through the struggle to dismantle them

(Gilbert, 2014; Fuster-Farfán, 2019).

3.3.3. Third Focus: The Energy Imperative

The sustainability challenge within Latin American housing policy has increasingly gravitated

towards technically oriented discourses. Current research tends to frame sustainability through

measurable variables—such as energy consumption, material life cycles, and urban resilience. This

focus reveals how the state, market, and built environment professions have responded to

overlapping crises: climate change, socio-spatial inequality, and infrastructure obsolescence. What

emerges is not only a technocratic vision of sustainability but a structural reappraisal of housing as a

key site of environmental governance.

strong associations between climate change, sustainability, environmental management, and social

4. Discussion

This study employed a two-stage bibliometric analysis to explore recent research on housing policy

in Latin America (2014–2024), with particular attention to three interconnected challenges: the

financialisation of housing, the persistence of socio-spatial inequalities, and the intensifying climate

crisis and its implications for sustainability. The first stage mapped the growing prominence and

### Page 19

entanglement of these themes; the second examined the internal structure and conceptual

trajectories of each.

Financialisation has emerged as a rapidly consolidating field, stemming from broader debates on

housing policy and urban economics, in line with concerns over the commodification of housing

(Aalbers & Christophers, 2014). Research centred on Mexico, Brazil and Chile explores the

expansion of mortgage markets targeting lower-income groups (Soederberg, 2015), state

programmes facilitating private capital inflows (Klink & Denaldi, 2014), and the influence of

financial actors such as REITs (Sanfelici & Halbert, 2019). These studies also highlight anxieties

around financial instability (Carrasco-Gallego, 2020; Duca et al., 2021), erosion of the right to the

city (Reyes, 2020), and the tensions between financial logic and housing access. While housing

prices remain a secondary concern, thematic maps indicate that regulatory frameworks and social

outcomes form the conceptual core (Molina et al., 2019).

Inequality remains a consistent axis. The targeted analysis reaffirms the centrality of social housing,

alongside persistent themes of poverty, informality, and segregation. These lines of inquiry reflect

the inadequacy of state responses to precariousness (Deneulin & Sánchez-Ancochea, 2018; Morales

Martínez, 2017; Matus Madrid et al., 2019). Key contributions include critiques of free housing

schemes (Gilbert, 2014) and assessments of neoliberal policy outcomes (Garreton, 2017; Fuster-

Farfán, 2019; Hidalgo Dattwyler et al., 2019), along with broader challenges to market-oriented

paradigms (Monkkonen, 2018; Cociña, 2021). The prominence of the right to the city as a motor

theme suggests a normative shift toward rights-based frameworks. Furthermore, the link between

housing quality, health, and human rights (Baker & Bentley, 2023; Camargo Sierra, 2020; Fowler &

Farrell, 2017) strengthens the view of housing as a key social determinant of well-being.

In the sustainability and climate domain, the analysis indicates a maturing field. The literature is

strongly focused on energy poverty and efficiency, with particular emphasis on the Chilean context

(Reyes et al., 2019; Schueftan et al., 2016; Pérez-Fargallo et al., 2018, 2017). These studies reflect

concern over environmental vulnerability and the paradox of policies that mitigate pollution but

worsen housing discomfort—clean air but colder homes. Thematic maps identify resilience, energy

efficiency, and life cycle as motor themes, with sustainable construction and social housing

classified as foundational. References to extreme events and natural disasters anchor these debates

within broader concerns about infrastructure and environmental justice (Sanfelici, 2016; Vargas-

Cuervo et al., 2024).

### Page 20

These three research fronts are not isolated; they are deeply intertwined. Financialisation

exacerbates inequality by turning housing into a speculative asset, while both dynamics tend to

undermine long-term sustainability goals. Energy poverty, the cornerstone of climate-related

debates, illustrates how socio-economic vulnerability is inextricably linked to environmental

fragility. Responding to the climate crisis requires not only technical fixes but also structural redress

to prevent retrofitting from benefiting only those who can afford it.

The findings from the second stage of analysis point to a system of interwoven crises that demand

an integrated approach. Financialisation, inequality, and sustainability are not discrete categories

but interdependent dimensions of urban governance. Collectively, the literature reveals a structural

tension: while transformative discourse proliferates, institutional responses remain anchored in

market rationalities, technocratic planning, and depoliticised notions of sustainability.

One of the most salient insights is the recurrence of inequality across all thematic clusters,

suggesting that housing policy has functioned less as a redistributive mechanism and more as a tool

of differentiated citizenship and spatial stratification. The increasing scholarly focus on financial

instruments—mortgages, investment funds, microfinance—reflects this reorientation, casting

housing as a financial asset and entrenching spatial injustice.

The climate crisis, moreover, is not simply an external stressor on housing systems; it is materially

and symbolically embedded within them. The emphasis on energy efficiency and resilience reveals

a pivot toward adaptive strategies—yet these often lack critical engagement with fossil capitalism

and environmental extractivism. In this context, sustainability risks becoming an alibi for expanding

market-led housing regimes.

Whilst methodologically rigorous, this study’s findings reveal a structural pathology within global

academic knowledge. The paper’s own acknowledgement of its linguistic bias is not a simple

limitation, but rather clear evidence of how the coloniality of knowledge (Quijano, 2007) operates.

The supremacy of English-language publications in databases such as Scopus and Web of Science

is not a neutral phenomenon; it is a filtering mechanism that validates knowledge conforming to the

language and epistemic priorities of the Global North. This process generates a form of

‘epistemicide’ (Santos, 2009), systematically erasing the vast intellectual production in Spanish and

Portuguese that is developed within and for the region. The findings, therefore, do not represent the

entire field of housing policy in Latin America, but rather a situated knowledge (Haraway, 1988)

that, without a decolonial critique, risks being presented as universal.

### Page 21

From this perspective, the lack of citational links amongst authors in the region is a symptom of an

internalised coloniality. The pursuit of validation in Northern journals reflects a logic wherein local

knowledge only acquires value upon being ‘exported’ and approved by the colonial centre.

Breaking this dynamic requires a conscious decolonising practice (Rivera Cusicanqui, 2010). This

is not merely a linguistic matter, but a political-academic project to weave networks of mutual

citation and strengthen regional editorial platforms. Fostering publication and dialogue in the

region's own languages is a fundamental step towards building an ‘ecology of knowledges’ (Santos,

2009). Such a practice would enable a sentipensar (‘feeling-thinking’) approach to housing policy,

one truly grounded in the territorial realities of Latin America (Escobar, 2014) rather than viewed

through the prism of a contemporary colonial language.

The field thus finds itself at a conceptual crossroads. Future research must go beyond describing

empirical patterns of these entangled crises to interrogate the ontological assumptions underlying

housing policy: What is housing for? Whom does it serve? And under what regimes of knowledge

and power is it governed?

5. Conclusions

This bibliometric review analysed 1,781 scholarly documents indexed in Scopus and Web of

Science on housing policy in Latin America from 2014 to 2024. Through a two-stage

methodological design, it first mapped the general field and then examined three interrelated axes in

depth: financialisation, inequality, and the climate crisis. The findings reveal a field that is

expanding in volume and becoming increasingly conceptually sophisticated, with clearly defined

thematic cores.

Financialisation emerges as an intensified frontier, where housing is reconfigured as a vehicle for

capital accumulation—often in tension with equity and the right to the city. Inequality remains

central, with a focus on informality, segregation, and socio-spatial exclusion, while rights-based and

gender-sensitive frameworks gain traction. Sustainability, meanwhile, bridges technical and

political concerns, focusing on energy poverty, climate vulnerability, and unequal resilience.

The study acknowledges limitations, including a bias toward English-language publications, limited

inclusion of grey literature, and difficulty in capturing theoretical innovation or political context. It

recommends expanding into multilingual literature, empirical research, and emerging technologies

### Page 22

such as tokenisation. Specifically, it is strongly recommend that future bibliometric studies in this

field incorporate regional databases like SciELO and Redalyc, and employ search queries in

Spanish and Portuguese. Such a multilingual and multi-database approach is essential to capture a

more complete and representative picture of the scholarly production on housing in Latin America,

moving beyond the anglocentric bias of global citation indexes. The need for policy frameworks

that confront exclusionary logics and environmental risk is also underscored, advocating cross-

sectoral approaches. Ultimately, housing emerges as a prism through which the structural

contradictions of Latin American urbanisation are rendered visible.
