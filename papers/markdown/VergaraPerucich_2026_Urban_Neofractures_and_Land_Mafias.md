Abstract: This article examines transformations in informal access to urban
land in Chile, marked by the emergence of mafias de la tierra (land mafias):
armed groups that organise land invasions and illegal subdivisions,
displacing long-standing housing struggles led by social movements and
political parties. This phenomenon produces what we term an urban
neofracture: a rupture through which land is converted into an informally
traded commodity, deepening socio-spatial exclusion and urban violence.
Drawing on the theory of the metabolic rift and foregrounding epistemic,
distributive and intergenerational injustices, the article analyses the actuarial
logic through which land mafias monetise vulnerability by estimating
eviction risk, enforcement lag and household financial exclusion, converting
structural uncertainty into predictable cash flow, and examines the practical
impossibility of state intervention. The study argues that these practices
configure a new urban scenario in which illegality competes with state
planning, eroding rights and consolidating transnational criminal circuits.
Keywords: Campamentización; informal settlements; land mafias; Chilean cities; informal
housing markets; migration

Across Latin America, the history of access to housing and urban land has for decades
been intertwined with the making of urban and peri-urban landscapes. Land occupations,
informal subdivisions and self-built housing have simultaneously operated as strategies of
survival and as forms of political contestation over the city, reshaping land use in ways that
overflowed formal planning frameworks while maintaining channels of communication
with political parties, trade unions and grassroots organisations (Duhau & Giglia, 2008;
Cáceres Seguel, 2025). In this context, the illegality of occupation was articulated with
normative horizons around the right to housing and to land, compelling the state to
respond through regularisation programmes, the provision of services and the gradual
incorporation of these settlements into the official urban landscape (Udvarhelyi, 2014).
Over the last decade, however, a different configuration of extralegal arrangements has
emerged in several Latin American cities, transforming urban and peri-urban landscapes in
distinct ways. In Chile, land invasions and campamentos (informal settlements) are no
longer only the outcome of residents’ collective mobilisation, but increasingly the product
of extralegal land uses promoted by criminal organisations that subdivide, sell and control
urban and rural land on the urban fringe. Analogous phenomena have been documented
in Colombia, through the tierreros (Ávila Martínez & Carmona Rojas, 2021), and in
Indonesia, via what are termed land mafias (Hansen, 2023), where illicit actors restructure
land use and capture rents through the organised occupation of public and private land.
These configurations exceed classical grammars of urban informality and form part of a
broader field of illicit activities that reconfigure land-use systems and patterns of land-
cover change.

The Chilean version of these formations, which we refer to as mafias de la tierra (land
mafias), introduces an urban neofracture within extralegal regimes of access to land. Unlike
earlier occupations mediated by residents’ organisations with formal or informal ties to
political parties and social movements, land mafias operate as criminal land enterprises
that systematically commodify the expansion of informal settlements, alter the
configuration of peri-urban landscapes and reorder relations between state, market and
community. Neofracture names precisely this methodological and ideological break: the
shift from a regime of politicised informality, which addressed the state in the language of
rights, to a regime of extralegal urbanisation governed by armed actors who extract rent
through illicit land sales and the violent management of everyday life.
Operationally, this article uses the term mafias de la tierra to refer to criminal arrangements
that display at least five distinctive features: (i) a stable organisational structure, hierarchical
or networked, that controls a portfolio of plots and includes differentiated roles (recruiters,
administrators, enforcers); (ii) the explicit or latent use of force—physical violence, threats,
destruction of dwellings or everyday harassment—as a mechanism of territorial
governance; (iii) pricing practices that impose payments per square metre, “entry fees” and
periodic charges presented as service or maintenance fees without any legal basis; (iv)
income models grounded in the capture of land rents and public transfers (subsidies, cash
benefits), converted into a permanent cash flow; and (v) linkages to other criminal circuits,
particularly drug markets and informal security services, which reinforce their coercive
capacity. We reserve the term mafias de la tierra for situations in which there is, at a
minimum, a stable organisational structure, the explicit or latent use of coercion, and the
systematic extraction of prices over occupied land. These attributes distinguish land mafias
from non-violent forms of informal intermediation associated with politically mediated
occupations, where the central actors were community leaders and party networks rather
than criminalised land enterprises.
The article is situated at the intersection of critical urban studies and land system science. It
asks how these organised extralegal land uses reconfigure Chilean urban and peri-urban
landscapes, reshape patterns of land-use change and informal densification, and strain
institutional capacities to govern territory. Drawing on an extended case study that
combines testimonial analysis, documentary review and multitemporal geospatial
interpretation, we propose the notion of urban neofracture as a tool for understanding the
territorial and political–ecological implications of land mafias, in dialogue with the theory
of the metabolic rift and with wider debates on informality, violence and land governance
in Latin America. The article extends recent work on property, violence and the
criminalisation of poverty. While Herbert and Brown’s (2006) analysis of the punitive
neoliberal city and Udvarhelyi’s (2014) account of homelessness in Budapest foreground
the tightening entanglements between urban governance, security and dispossession, and
Harrington & Baka’s (2026) study of the “hardening and hollowing out” of private property
theorises rentiership as a planetary regime of enclosure, this paper brings these concerns
to bear on the informal housing circuits of Latin American cities. In doing so, it situates its
theorisation of illegal urban rentierism within Southern scholarship that has long insisted

that capitalism and property regimes operate distinctly where privatisation and enclosure
remain ongoing processes rather than completed histories. Ghertner (2015) argues that
frameworks derived from mature private housing markets fail in ‘much of the world’, where
land is still being enclosed, titled and commodified, and where housing outcomes are
shaped less by market exchange than by contested processes of dispossession; Harvey’s
(2003) account of accumulation by dispossession similarly identifies enclosure as a
continuing, not merely originary, moment of capitalist urbanisation. Read through this lens,
land mafias are not anomalous actors within an otherwise formal market: they are agents
of an ongoing primitive accumulation who enclose public and private land, convert
occupancy into tradable claims, and extract rent precisely where private housing markets
do not reach — constituting rental and sale markets that are internal to, rather than
derivative of, the informal city. It dialogues with Narayan’s (2026) treatment of slum
evictions through labour, capture and value, and with Tyler and Skeggs’ (2026)
conceptualisation of necroeconomic suffocation, by showing how land mafias transform
campamentización into a site where coercive property regimes and slow violence are
operationalised through illegal markets rather than formal planning. The proposed notion
of urban neofracture, read through the lens of the metabolic rift, thus contributes a
Southern, slum-based vantage point from which to rethink property, informality and urban
justice in critical geography.
More specifically, Tyler and Skeggs (2026) theorise “necroeconomic suffocation” as a
regime in which the poor are slowly killed by the withdrawal of social infrastructure and the
multiplication of fees, fines and market demands that consume what little income they
possess. Narayan (2026) analyses how slum evictions in the Global South operate through
the capture of labour value and the confiscation of accumulated urban assets from
communities. This article contributes to both conversations by showing how land mafias
operationalise necroeconomic suffocation through illegal market mechanisms rather than
state policy: the fees, instalment payments and “service charges” extracted by criminal
organisations function as a private tax on dispossession, consuming the labour income of
migrant households whilst simultaneously denying them the legal standing that would
allow them to challenge their exploitation.
Urban neofracture is developed here in explicit dialogue with adjacent conceptual
frameworks elaborated in detail in the analytical sections below. It departs from criminal
governance scholarship (Arias, 2009; Grajales, 2011), which foregrounds non-state armed
actors as producers of territorial order, to designate instead the destruction of that
negotiated order and its replacement by extractive market logic. It differs from Wacquant's
(2008) advanced marginality — structural dispossession produced by state retrenchment
— by identifying the specific agents and mechanisms that colonise the resulting void in the
domain of land access; and it qualifies Roy's (2005) theorisation of informality as a
planning category by naming a rupture within extralegal practices rather than their
selective recognition by the state. Against peripheral urbanisation scholarship (Caldeira,
2017; Parnell & Oldfield, 2014), neofracture designates the historically specific moment at
which criminal enterprises displace community-based governance as the primary mode of
extralegal city-making. Three minimum indicators operationalise the concept empirically;

A descriptive quadripolar model of housing and access to urban land in Chilean cities
Figure 1 is a heuristic analytical device derived from field materials; it maps ideal-type
positions rather than statistical distributions and is not a direct representation of any single
empirical case. The vertical axis designates the degree of tenure formalisation — from
legally registered, institutionally backed arrangements (upper pole) to extralegal,
unregistered or openly illegal ones (lower pole). The horizontal axis designates the mode
of governance and mediation — from predominantly state-institutional arrangements (left
pole) to non-state, criminal or autonomous community-based ones (right pole). These two
dimensions are independent: a settlement may be highly informalised in tenure terms
whilst being governed by an organised community rather than by criminal actors, placing it
in the lower-left rather than the lower-right quadrant. The intersection of both axes yields
four quadrants that should not be understood as watertight compartments, but as relative
positions within a field of forces: households may move between quadrants over time, for
instance when they shift from formal rental in arrears (I) to a campamento controlled by
land mafias (II), or from a regularised occupation (II) to a state-led housing programme
(IV).

The upper-right quadrant (I) integrates formal and informal possibilities, concentrating
most of the strategies historically deployed to secure urban land and housing. Here we
locate institutional alternatives such as state programmes, NGO and foundation initiatives,
market-based access, legally constituted rentals, temporary occupations of private and
public space (for instance, rukos), and public–private partnerships. Arrangements with a
certain degree of informality are also included, insofar as they tend to “spill over” towards
quadrant II, where informal options dominate. Empirically, this quadrant encompasses
formal market purchases with mortgage financing in consolidated Santiago districts such
as Providencia and Las Condes, where households access land through regulated credit
and registered titles.
Quadrant II represents a non-state realm in which powers parallel to the state, or public
and private institutions operating outside the legal framework, generate informal
“solutions” to the housing deficit. It includes actions by interest groups and organisations
that intervene by overstepping the law, whether through direct action or support for non-
institutional initiatives. This quadrant is conceived as an arena of competing social powers
and individual and collective agency, producing an anarchic and shifting cartography. It is
here that recent forms of illegal occupation and allocation of land for housing emerge,
including the activities of land mafias. These are newly consolidated and organised forms
of occupation by armed groups—gangs or criminal organisations—that seize public or
private land and subdivide it into plots for households they relocate or attract, charging for
the land on which dwellings are built. Such practices fall under the label loteos brujos
(“witch” subdivisions) (de la Fuente, 2023), and recent estimates suggest that around 8% of
Chilean campamento residents obtained their plot by paying illegal intermediaries

(TECHO–CES, 2023). Analytically, we distinguish between community-based informal
intermediation and land mafias, defined by their criminal organisational structure,
systematic use of coercion and a business model centred on the capture of land rents.
Quadrant II is not internally homogeneous; it encompasses a continuum of extralegal
arrangements that differ in their relationship to legal norms, to community organisation
and to the use of coercion. Sub-position II-A designates historically established
community-managed occupations (tomas): politicised, collectively organised, and
operating with an at least implicit claim on state regularisation. Sub-position II-B comprises
non-coercive informal intermediation — loteos brujos without armed governance,
brokered by local operators who exploit regulatory gaps but do not systematically use
violence. Sub-position II-C designates mafia-controlled campamentos: criminal
organisations that combine territorial coercion, systematic rent extraction and articulation
to wider illegal circuits. It is at and beyond the threshold between II-B and II-C that the
neofracture operates. This continuum also illuminates the informal economy in informal
settlements: the economic activity of residents in sub-positions II-A and II-B — small
commerce, domestic labour, informal services — represents the survival economy of
informality documented in the existing literature (Portes & Haller, 2005); it is analytically
distinct from, though practically entangled with, the criminal “privatisation” of land access
that characterises sub-position II-C.
On the pricing of plots: "They told us it was a thousand dollars for the plot and then three
hundred a month for 'maintenance'. We didn't know what that meant — who was
maintaining anything? But if you didn't pay, they came at night" (Resident T., female
community leader, February 2024, metropolitan fringe). This testimony is consistent with
the pricing structures documented in TECHO-CES (2023) and with testimonies collected
across eight additional campamentos during the 2023–2025 fieldwork cycle, all of which
reported initial payments between USD 500 and USD 1,500 for plot access, followed by
monthly charges framed as "service" or "security" fees.
Two major turning points severely strained the housing-access formulas implemented in
Chile since the late twentieth century: the Haitian–Colombian–Venezuelan migration wave
and the COVID-19 pandemic. Both processes encouraged self-provision and alternative
responses to the demand for land and housing that were not led by traditional social or
political movements. New illegal suppliers of land were faster and apparently more
“effective”, as they bypassed state bureaucracy and openly challenged legality, confronting
a state whose agencies neither applied the same criteria nor acted with sufficient resolve.
As in peripheral areas affected by drug trafficking, the “firepower” of armed groups began
to substitute the allocative and protective role of the state, rendering sovereignty and the
rule of law largely invisible and giving rise to an alternative land market that fractured
previously more uniform strategies of access to land and housing.
This article foregrounds the role of land mafias in this quadrant. Their practices echo those
of tierreros in Colombia (Ávila Martínez & Carmona Rojas, 2021; Botero Ospina, 2022) and
armed land brokers in Indonesia (Hansen, 2023): groups of individuals who, in the presence

of legal voids and state anomie, organise to perpetrate fraud, forge property documents
and carry out de facto expropriations that facilitate occupations in Chile and elsewhere.
Quadrant III represents spaces where strong state or private regulatory control coexists
with the effective exclusion of low-income groups from both formal and informal housing
access. The “ideal type” here is not normatively desirable: it designates territories whose
tight governance forecloses popular appropriation without substituting redistributive
mechanisms. In the Chilean context this quadrant is empirically present — port security
perimeters, restricted ecological reserves, decommissioned industrial zones under
municipal authority — but it represents a horizon of exclusion rather than a model of
collective land stewardship. The normative aspiration that strong public regulation might
instead be turned towards the collective interest belongs not to this quadrant’s empirical
description but to the horizon of re-politicisation taken up in the Discussion.
The implementation of neoliberal policies in Latin America produced, where most
“successful”, precisely the kind of situation described in quadrant III. It also created land
and housing markets that operated through the formal financial system and through
suppliers and demanders directly dependent on the state. In Chile, private developers
came to produce social housing for beneficiaries able to “choose” a dwelling armed with a
state voucher. The allocation of land for self-build schemes was progressively abandoned,
and the value of individual saving and personal effort was re-signified, displacing collective
mobilisation and community-based struggles.
The quadripolar model was generated through an iterative process: inductively, from the
systematisation of interview material, geospatial evidence and secondary sources;
deductively, through assessment of its fit with the wider literature on informality, criminal
governance and housing policy. Its robustness rests on three criteria: logical consistency
(the four quadrants are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive of the theoretical space
defined by the axes); empirical calibration (the observable indicators in Table 1 allow
documented cases to be assigned to positions reliably); and explanatory productivity (the
model makes visible gradients and hybrid configurations — such as the continuum within
Quadrant II — that categorical typologies obscure). The qualitative validation procedures
through which the model was tested against the empirical material — independent
double-coding, triangulation across data sources, and validation with field-based experts
— are reported in the methodological section.
Tensions between an omnipotent market, protected by a privatising state, and those
seeking decent housing through institutional and, when necessary, informal means,
generated stigmatising narratives around campamentización and areas occupied by social
housing. A discursive triad—“young person (or poblador) – delinquent – marginal”—came
to characterise large segments of the urban population. This new social settlement was
organised around state withdrawal and the emergence of citizens effectively denied the
city and the benefits of urban life. Those who did not inhabit the segregated spaces
provided by the state, and instead occupied public or private land irregularly, were

increasingly treated as outside the scope of urbanistic and property rights and thus as
inhabiting a condition of de facto rightlessness (Abramo, 2012; Wacquant, 2008).
Metabolic rift, actuarial logic and circuits
In Case A, multitemporal analysis of satellite imagery spanning 2014 to 2024 documents a
conversion of approximately 18.4 hectares of agricultural and peri-urban open land to
informal residential fabric. Plot density increased from 12 to 42 dwellings per hectare over
the observation period, whilst the normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) for the
site and its immediate surroundings declined by 0.31 units — a loss consistent with the
elimination of productive agricultural cover and the removal of riparian vegetation along
an adjacent watercourse. These material transformations are not incidental: they reflect the
anticipatory subdivision logic of land mafias, whose business model depends on rapid
densification and the progressive monetisation of each new wave of plot sales. The
ecological rupture they produce is thus a direct function of the extractive temporality
embedded in their business model.
The metabolic rift, as elaborated by Foster (1999) from Marx's analysis of capitalist
agriculture, designates the structural breach that capitalist production opens in the socio-
ecological cycles through which societies reproduce themselves and their environments —
a breach that cannot be healed through market adjustment because it is constitutive of the
accumulation dynamic itself. Clark and Foster (2009) extended the concept to imperial
resource extraction; more recently, urban scholars have mobilised it to diagnose the
contradictions of capitalist urbanisation (Hidalgo Dattwyler et al., 2022; Figueirôa-Ferreira
et al., 2022), showing how the commodification of land, water and infrastructure ruptures
the metabolic flows through which urban territories sustain their populations.
We extend this reasoning to the domain of housing access. The urban neofracture
constitutes a metabolic rift in the social ecology of land: the illegal commodification of
plots by criminal organisations severs the connections between territory, social
reproduction and political capacity that — however tenuously — linked campamentización
to rights-based claims on the state. This rift does not merely redistribute access to housing;
it reorganises the flows of vulnerability, rent and dispossession in ways that foreclose the
very social actors capable of demanding redistributive repair. Three contributions of the
concept are particularly salient here: its intergenerational sensitivity (decisions taken under
criminal extortion condition the housing trajectories and environmental exposures of
future residents); its systemic scope (land, water, labour and political participation are
articulated as moments of an expanded socio-ecological metabolism); and its capacity to
show how material rupture and institutional rupture reinforce each other — land mafias
operate precisely at the intersection where both are simultaneously present.
By actuarial logic we designate the calculative rationality through which land mafias
monetise vulnerability: they price access to plots by estimating the risk of eviction, the
likely lag in state enforcement, and the degree of financial exclusion of target households,
converting structural uncertainty into a predictable cash flow. This logic is actuarial in the
technical sense — it assigns monetary values to probabilities of institutional intervention
— and it is applied systematically: initial plot fees, periodic maintenance charges, payments
for falsified documents and tariffs for access to utilities are all calibrated to what

households can pay and what the criminal enterprise can extract before regulatory
pressure materialises. The result is a business model that treats dispossession not as an
accident but as a structural resource.
In the upper-left quadrant (IV), formal options are concentrated. This quadrant represents
the ideal neoliberal configuration: two principal forms of subsidised housing—directly via
vouchers or indirectly through private credit for social housing in a market created for low-
income groups, and subsidies linked to mortgage finance that bind households to the
financial system.
To make the model empirically operational, we associate with each quadrant a bundle of
observable indicators (Table 1). Quadrant I groups access arrangements mediated by the
formal market: registered sales, authorised subdivisions, legally registered rental contracts,
mortgages and insurance requiring bankable solvency. Quadrant II comprises situations
lacking full legal backing: subdivisions without approved layouts, absence of registered
titles, irregular service connections, cash payments without contracts and the presence of
coercive or criminal devices in land governance. Quadrant III functions as an ideal type of
strongly regulated spaces where access is decommodified or tightly constrained for
reasons of general interest (for example, protected environmental areas, critical
infrastructure, land reserves), while quadrant IV brings together housing policies and
assisted programmes with formal tenure instruments, subsidies and explicit state
sponsorship. Some arrangements—for instance, informal rentals in formally titled dwellings
—sit on the boundary between quadrants I and II; the diagram is designed precisely to
make such grey zones visible as tensions between formal legality and actually existing
practices.
Scope and limitations
This is a theory-building extended case study; it advances analytical propositions rather
than statistically representative findings. The selection of Case A and the additional
campamentos is theoretically driven — settlements were chosen because they exemplify
distinct positions in the proposed typology — and, whilst the typology itself has been
qualitatively validated through the procedures reported in the methodological section, the
findings cannot be extrapolated to the national distribution of campamento types without
further large-scale research. All testimonies cited derive from the existing interview
database, systematised through abductive thematic coding; no new fieldwork was required
for the revision. The fragmentation and opacity of administrative sources make it
impossible, in the current state of knowledge, to reconstruct robust aggregate rent flows
associated with land mafias or to estimate their weight relative to other contemporary
forms of informal occupation.

Methodological approach and operationalisation of quadrants
This article draws on empirical materials generated within a set of state-funded research
projects in Chile that examine the housing crisis from the vantage point of critical
geography. Rather than a single, self-contained study, the analysis mobilises an
aggregated dataset that combines multitemporal geospatial evidence with an extended
qualitative case study. This enables us to track how land mafias and informal housing
regimes reconfigure urban and peri-urban landscapes, while remaining grounded in the
situated experiences of those who inhabit them.
The design is mixed-methods. First, we undertake a geospatial analysis of land-use change
based on sequences of high-resolution satellite imagery and orthophotographs (2014–
2024). For each site, campamento footprints and subsequent enlargements were manually
digitised in Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS), starting from the first
continuous perimeter of dwellings and progressively incorporating new fronts and infill.
From these polygons we derived key metrics—surface area, rate of expansion and plot
density—which underpin Figures 2 and 3. Additional indicators capture (i) the conversion
of agricultural, vacant or protected land into informal residential fabric (hectares
transformed per period); (ii) the intensity of occupation, estimated as plots per hectare; and
(iii) the directionality of growth (peripheral expansion versus internal densification). These
parsimonious metrics position campamentos controlled by land mafias within the broader
continuum of informal urbanisation and open discussion of their implications for landscape
fragmentation and unequal socio-environmental exposure. All geospatial layers are made
available in simplified form as supplementary material to ensure reproducibility without
compromising research ethics.
Given the sensitivity of the phenomenon, involving criminal organisations and episodes of
violence, we implemented stringent safeguards of spatial confidentiality. Figures and
descriptions do not disclose precise coordinates, campamento names or explicit references
to particular municipalities. Instead, we use generic labels and broad locational descriptors
(for example, “northern periphery of a medium-sized city” or “urban–metropolitan fringe”),
and the cartographies are simplified and, where necessary, slightly displaced or aggregated
to prevent the identification of individual plots and streets. The decision not to release
exact coordinates or raw vector files responds to security and ethical considerations in

Qualitative fieldwork was carried out between 2019 and 2025, with more intensive data

actors, episodes of violence, and relations with public institutions and security forces.

conflicts, and public documents relating to disputes over land occupation. Where possible,
we examined cadastral and ownership records to verify the legal status of plots, as well as
local land-use plans and zoning instruments that shape land values. However, the

knowledge, to reconstruct robust aggregate rent flows associated with land mafias or to

Contested control: land mafias, state incapacity and coercive access to land
The practical impossibility of securing a legal place to live – whether in the form of a plot
for (self-)building or as access to social housing – is one of the defining features of the new
poverty regimes of the Global South, and has intensified in Chile since 2011 (Ramírez,
2021). It excludes individuals, families and communities from a fair, democratic and
inclusive use of public resources and goods, including the possibility of inhabiting territory
without the prior condition of having sufficient money to pay for it (Fernández & Salinas,
2012). By “practical impossibility” we refer not to an abstract lack of opportunities, but to a
dense web of legal and institutional constraints. Legally, the reinforced protection of
property rights and due process narrows the scope for early action on irregular
subdivisions as long as no final court ruling exists; police powers are designed to pursue
individualised offences rather than dismantle organisational arrangements that resemble
long-standing forms of informality; and emergency decrees or states of exception are
extraordinary instruments, politically costly and selectively applied. Institutionally,
fragmented budgets, weak coordination between housing ministries, municipalities,
prosecutors and police forces, together with the risks of local capture and corruption,
undermine the capacity to sustain long-term interventions. It is this chain of legal and
organisational restrictions, more than simple “lack of political will”, that produces a
practical impossibility and enables land mafias to operate as a quasi-state on the ground.
“ We live with the permanent fear of eviction” is a phrase that recurs among families living
in a campamento on the periphery of Viña del Mar and is echoed in other settlements in
that city and elsewhere. This captures a situation one might associate with the third
quadrant, which, as discussed, is defined by the defence of public or private property rights
and the criminalisation of residents’ movements – including those whose actions still follow
traditional political repertoires. Today, however, it also marks a distinctive feature of the
second quadrant, linked to the production of the neofracture: the chain of illegalities and
the criminal nature of land mafias obstruct the operation of political–institutional
arrangements that once offered explicit support to land claims made by vulnerable
populations. In this context, part of recent campamentización – the proliferation of
campamentos – can be read as informal claims to shelter, expressed through the de facto,
peaceful or violent occupation of urban space, affecting both public and private forms of
property.
Testimonies collected in several Chilean cities describe a similar script. Armed groups arrive
unexpectedly at vacant land or at a public or private plot, fence it off or mark boundaries
to separate it from its surroundings; once this enclosure is in place, buses bring in families,
usually migrants, who are assigned individual plots. Figure 2, taken from an image
captured in 2020, illustrates some peculiarities of this kind of mafia-led campamentización.

a short period, and there was little evidence of an external actor designing the spatial
layout. In Case A, by contrast, armed groups played a clear organising role and exercised
often-intense symbolic violence, for example by controlling entry and exit points to the

In Case A, most campamento residents were migrants. Similar operations appeared in

relations with surrounding neighbourhoods and institutions. Land mafias regulate who
may enter and leave, who gains access to basic infrastructure and services such as
electricity and drinking water, and even the size of the plot sold. They perpetrate offences
including fraud, forgery of property and ownership documents, and de facto
expropriations designed to facilitate occupation.
Beyond Case A, the systematisation of interviews and TECHO–CES data reveals consistent
patterns in prices, recruitment and income flows in these settlements. Typically there is an
initial payment for the “right to a plot”, followed by regular instalments and extra charges
for services or falsified documents. Recruitment operates through ethnic, labour and
religious networks, as well as targeted social media campaigns, aimed largely at migrant
households excluded from the formal housing market. Although clandestinity makes it
difficult to quantify amounts involved, the evidence suggests that these mafias function as
illegal real-estate firms, monetising every step of the process.

What, then, differentiates the second from the third quadrant? In both, formal guarantees
of property and a punitive approach to informality loom large. In the third quadrant,
however, the outcome is a space of strict control and relative residential “emptiness”: land
is tightly regulated or reserved for other uses, and informal claims are largely kept at bay.
In the second quadrant, by contrast, control is disputed: the state’s practical incapacity to
act – produced by the legal and institutional constraints outlined above – opens room for
land mafias to step in, organise occupation, impose their own rules and extract rents from
those with no other viable route to shelter. It is precisely in this disjuncture, where formal
rights remain on paper but are not effectively enforced, that the neofracture emerges.
Campamentización as evidence of a neofracture
To examine the processes that may emerge in the second quadrant in relation to the
proposed neofracture, we draw on the notion of the metabolic rift developed in political
ecology, political economy, critical geography and related environmental fields to diagnose
the contradictions of capitalism (Bahers & Giacchè, 2019; Clark & Foster, 2009; Clausen &
Clark, 2005; Foster, 1999). This conceptual lineage has already been mobilised in urban
analysis, often in dialogue with systems theory (Hidalgo Dattwyler et al., 2022; Figueirôa-
Ferreira et al., 2022).
The neofracture signals the impact, within the housing and land sector, of hierarchically
organised armed groups that operate as epistemic communities in the sense established
by Haas (1992): networks that share a codified body of practical knowledge — in this case,
techniques for identifying occupiable land, subdividing plots, pricing access, forging
documentation and managing coercion — and that reproduce and transmit this expertise
across territories and operations. Their business is selling land for housing in contexts of
acute vulnerability and precarity. Their practices no longer belong to a recognisable
repertoire of political struggle, nor can they be straightforwardly read as expressions of
class conflict over urban land or access to housing. Precisely for that reason, they intensify
exclusion and socio-spatial segregation among vulnerable groups: the illegality of the
entire chain of actions required to market land and plots makes it extremely difficult for
political, institutional or para-institutional actors to intervene in favour of demands that
cannot be satisfied by formal channels in the short term.
The concept of urban neofracture is analytically distinct from several related frameworks. It
differs from criminal governance (Arias, 2009; Grajales, 2011) in that the latter emphasises
the capacity of non-state armed actors to produce order and negotiate territorial authority
18

### Page 19

with the state; neofracture, by contrast, designates precisely the destruction of negotiated
order — including within the informal register — and its replacement by an extractive
market logic that forecloses political mediation. It differs from Wacquant’s (2008) advanced
marginality in that the latter describes structural dispossession produced by state
retrenchment, whereas neofracture identifies the specific agents and mechanisms that fill
that void in the domain of land access. It also differs from Roy’s (2005) conceptualisation of
informality as a planning category, because neofracture designates not a state’s selective
recognition of extralegal practices but a rupture within those practices themselves. In
relation to the broader literature on peripheral urbanisation (Caldeira, 2017; Parnell &
Oldfield, 2014), neofracture is not a general condition of informality but a specific mutation
within it: it presupposes the prior existence of a politicised informal field and names the
moment when criminal actors displace community-based governance of that field. A case
qualifies as a neofracture when the following minimum indicators concur: (i) a stable
criminal organisational structure controlling a portfolio of plots; (ii) the systematic use of
coercion — physical violence, threats or harassment — as a mode of territorial governance;
and (iii) the regularised extraction of rents from occupants without legal basis. These
thresholds are operationalised through the observable indicators in Table 1.
We use the term urban neofracture not merely as an evocative metaphor, but as an
analytical construct composed of three observable dimensions. First, an organisational
fracture, whereby criminal actors displace residents' organisations and partisan mediations
as the primary managers of access to land. Second, a distributive fracture, visible in the
extraction of exceptional rents from households already excluded from the formal market
and from housing policies, thereby consolidating new forms of economic dependence.
Third, an institutional fracture, manifested in the rupture of the historical channels that
linked campamentización to struggles for housing, political parties and the state. We speak
of neofracture when these three dimensions co-constitute one another around mafia-like
circuits of access to the city, reconfiguring the field of urban forces.
This configuration crystallises at least three forms of (in)justice: epistemic, intergenerational
and distributive–economic. Together they produce the neofracture and make the
metabolic rift framework particularly pertinent, given its focus on how capitalist production
opens a lasting breach in the social metabolism. In the domain of urban land, capitalism
accelerates depletion — understood here, following Foster (1999) and Clark and Foster
(2009), as the progressive exhaustion of the socio-ecological capacities of territory: the loss
of productive soils, vegetation cover and hydrological function of the kind documented in
Case A, together with the erosion of the social capacities (organisation, mutual aid, political
mediation) through which communities sustain habitability — by subjecting allocation to
speculative dynamics and differentiating supply according to households’ capacity to
participate in the market. Economic barriers push the poorest towards locations marked by
negative externalities: environmental risks, weak access to services and infrastructures, and
fragile social protections. These conditions shape not only the lives of present generations
but also those of future inhabitants.

From the standpoint of distributive injustice, speculation and structural capital
concentration in cities generate continuous urban expansion over rural areas and the
displacement of low-income groups towards peripheral locations. Epistemic injustices arise
when residents are denied the possibility of participating in the resolution of their own
problems: they are not recognised by the state and its institutions as interlocutors or peers,
but treated as passive recipients of decisions taken in technical, economic or political
arenas. All this deepens the neofracture. The state—and other political actors—is
ontologically ill-equipped to engage with situations that systematically benefit actors
operating outside the law and openly violating private property, a cornerstone of the
contemporary nation-state. Property rights are a foundational pillar of the modern state
and its legal order, insofar as the state acts as political regulator and ultimate decision-
making authority (Jovanoski & Rustemi, 2021; Luhmann, 2015).
Invoking the metabolic rift also allows us to move beyond classic frameworks of informality
or urban political ecology focused primarily on the distribution of environmental risks and
benefits. First, it introduces an explicitly intergenerational sensitivity, highlighting how
current decisions on land use and access to the city condition the future reproducibility of
territories and their communities. Second, it compels us to think of land and housing as
moments within an expanded socio-ecological metabolism—articulating energy, water and
labour circuits—rather than as simple consumption goods or financial assets. Third, it
illuminates the coupling of material rupture (in value chains, infrastructures and land-
occupation patterns) and institutional rupture (in norms, rights and state capacities),
showing that land mafias operate precisely in those interstices where both fractures
reinforce each other. In this way, the urban neofracture becomes an analytical key for
reading the combined effects of the illegal commodification of land and the
reconfiguration of relations between state, market and community.
From the perspective of the state, the actions of these groups severely damage the
metabolic links between society and nature, transforming land into a commodity traded in
opaque markets that can trigger speculative spirals and undermine the social function of
urban space. Housing, in turn, becomes a fully tradable good, subject to direct occupation,
indirect exploitation or pure speculation. Addressing this situation requires a re-
politicisation of access to land—and therefore to housing—through concrete measures
that restore its social function and narrow the rift by reconnecting urban space with natural
and social systems. This, in turn, calls for participatory planning and the recognition of
social movements and organised residents as legitimate interlocutors.
As noted above, the neofracture associated with armed groups emerges from the rise of
circuits of praxis that are not anchored in the political sphere and do not primarily express
class struggles or responses to socio-spatial exclusion, even if they can occasionally
appropriate that language. Rather, they are violent operations structured through illegal
market logics: processes of buying and selling that challenge property rights and bypass
both formal and, increasingly, traditional informal mechanisms of access to land and
housing. The resulting gap marks a widening distance between two strategic articulations
20

### Page 21

of land and housing struggles in Chilean, Latin American and, likely, other Global South
cities. The fragile equilibrium between political action and a minimal ethical justification for
the routes chosen to meet social demands—no matter how urgent—is thereby broken.
Political parties and state and non-state institutions face an ethical dilemma that prevents
them from actively supporting movements that challenge property rights through criminal
practices of illicit appropriation.
Debates over the validity, scope and contemporary relevance of property rights are thus
inseparable from reflection on state powers and dynamics. The neoliberal utopia
represented by Quadrant III in Figure 1 unfolds in a context of absolute supremacy of
property rights over all others. In such a scenario, residents’ movements and other informal
initiatives—including land mafias—are tightly constrained. At the same time, the state
often lacks land reserves for redistribution; such spaces become the object of real-estate
speculation and function as extensions of individual market power, according to each
actor’s position in the field.
The risks posed by land mafias can, paradoxically, further intensify the defence of both
public and private property through the absolute privatisation of vacant land, preventing it
from becoming a common good. This tendency is frequently justified by the argument that
privatisation guarantees better care and protection of assets by their owners (Algara
Sánchez de las Matas, 2008; Hardin, 1992). In this context, the neofracture does not merely
name a new configuration of informality; it also exposes the political and ethical limits of
contemporary land and housing regimes, revealing the depth of the metabolic rift that
structures urban life under contemporary capitalism.
Land mafias: an ekphrastic reading of the present and future of campamentización in
Chile?
It is necessary to examine the possible associations between struggles for housing and the
operation of land mafias within the broader context of urban violence. This form of
violence has been defined in multiple ways (Agnew, 2010; Almeida, 2022; Body-Gendrot,
1995; Butler, 2004; F. Carrión, 2002; F. M. Carrión, 2008; Del Olmo, 2000; Saborío, 2018),
which has clear implications for how it is measured and for the policies designed to
combat or mitigate it, given that a violent city directly affects the level and quality of life of
its inhabitants.
The absence of a shared perspective on the origins, effects, externalities, and the respective
roles of the state, institutions and civil society—as key protagonists of this form of violence
—has contributed to a certain ambiguity in conceptual and definitional terms. This, in turn,
extends to how much weight is given to cultural, sociopolitical, geographical and
sociodemographic factors when explaining the causes and evolution of such phenomena.
For example, Sophie Body-Gendrot (1995) underscored the combined influence of market
logics, privatisation dynamics and institutional arrangements as spatial operators (Lussault,
2015) of urban violence. Her work exemplifies a recurrent tendency in the social sciences
21

### Page 22

and humanities to frame violence as a predictable spatial solution to unmet demands for
urban land—an approach that, in her view, helps explain the specific characteristics of the
uprisings in Paris and Los Angeles in the final decade of the last century.
A coherent approach to the assemblages of land mafias must treat as integral to the
problem a set of interactions which, on the one hand, deepen the stigmas and stereotypes
through which the most vulnerable are represented and, on the other, position them as
victims both of organised armed groups and of a state that is epistemologically unable to
recognise this mode of claiming urban land. It has been argued that this difficulty is a
fundamental component of the neofractura—the new fracture—used here to
conceptualise the emergence of a recent alternative route for meeting demands for urban
land.
From the end of the previous decade, roughly between 2010 and 2020, in a context
marked by social uprisings and the pandemic in Chile, illegal occupations orchestrated by
land mafias took place, with particular intensity during the pandemic, when on-the-ground
surveillance was weakened by other priorities deemed more urgent. A common pattern
can be identified in the mafias’ modus operandi: first, a selection process identified both
ends of the chain—on the one hand, a plot of land and, on the other, potential occupants.
This was followed by operations such as the demolition of existing boundaries, the creation
of an access road—sometimes involving heavy machinery—and, in some cases, the
intervention of the urban electricity network. Once the land had been prepared, it was
subdivided among prospective occupants who were then transported by vehicle, more or
less simultaneously, to the future encampment. The process was completed with the
installation of a perimeter fence and armed lookout posts that controlled the number of
residents, preventing the arrival of others who had presumably not participated in the prior
payments required to finance this mobilisation.

The analytical portability of urban neofracture depends on the co-presence of four
structural conditions: an acute housing deficit that cannot be met by formal market or
state supply; regulatory and enforcement gaps that allow extralegal actors to operate with
relative impunity; large migrant or displaced populations whose exclusion from formal
tenure makes them captive consumers of criminalised land markets; and the presence of
armed groups with organisational capacity to manage territory and extract rents over time.
These conditions are empirically identifiable — and in some cases intensifying — across
urban Latin America. In Colombia, the tierreros documented by Ávila Martínez and
Carmona Rojas (2021) and Botero Ospina (2022) exhibit the organisational fracture and
rent extraction that characterise neofracture, though without the same anticipatory
subdivision logic observed in Chilean cases. In Brazil, milícias and grilagem operations
(Müller, 2024) combine territorial coercion with parallel property markets at a scale that
exceeds the Chilean case. In Mexico, paracaidistas and lotificadores operate through
networks that increasingly overlap with organised crime circuits (Connolly, 2009). These
parallels suggest that neofracture captures a broader mutation in the articulation of
informality, violence and urban land production — one that requires comparative
investigation rather than case-by-case description.
Urban neofracture does not announce the birth of capitalist informality, nor is it simply its
latest iteration. It designates a specific and historically located mutation: the displacement
of politically mediated access — in which community organisations, party networks and
residents' movements negotiated with the state in the language of rights — by a criminal
enterprise logic that operates through coercion and extracts value from the basic need for
shelter. This mutation is not intrinsic to informality as such; it is the product of a specific
conjuncture: the intensification of the housing deficit beyond what formal and traditional
informal channels can absorb, the scale of migration flows that produce large populations
with no prior territorial affiliation, the weakening of enforcement capacity in peri-urban
peripheries, and the expansion of criminal networks with organisational capacity and
capital to invest in territorial control. In this sense, neofracture is historically specific and
conjuncturally produced — it is not inevitable but contingent, and understanding its
conditions is the first step towards countering it.
The metabolic rift framework is deployed here as a diagnostic instrument, not as a counsel
of despair. Foster's own analysis makes clear that rifts are not metaphysically permanent;
they are produced by specific social relations and can, in principle, be addressed through
the transformation of those relations. The re-politicisation we advocate does not propose a
nostalgic return to the state forms or community organisations of a prior era, nor does it
rely on the neoliberal state acting against its own class interests. Rather, it designates a
project of reconstructing the channels of political mediation that land mafias have
fractured — rebuilding the capacity of residents' organisations, housing movements and
planning institutions to articulate demands in the language of rights and to negotiate
collective outcomes for urban land. This is not a repair of the rift within existing
institutional frameworks; it is a transformation of the social relations that produced the rift,
24

### Page 25

achieved through participatory planning, public land banking, anti-corruption mechanisms
and the formal recognition of organised communities as legitimate interlocutors of
housing policy. The metabolic rift provides the diagnosis; re-politicisation is the horizon of
repair.

The insertion of these spaces into national and transnational criminal circuits, combined
with rapidly intensifying social stigmatisation that increasingly casts them as precarious
ghettos operating in the state’s shadow, functions as a powerful mechanism of
segregation, marginalisation and exclusion. Residents are unable to access state support or
obtain a regular address through which they might integrate into the financial system and
formal labour market. The direct outcome—alongside other circumstances and reasons—is
the incorporation of a significant portion of the population into the informal economy or,
in some cases, into overtly criminal circuits, including micro-trafficking, participation in
theft, and various forms of illegal trade.

control who sells what in the neighbourhood. The drugs arrived six months after the first
families. It's the same network" (NGO housing professional, anonymised, September 2023,
metropolitan area).
The detachment of populations from formal labour markets and from public and private
mechanisms of inclusion gives rise to a parallel city that is highly susceptible to being co-
opted by alternative sources of power. This process is facilitated by entrenched conditions
of inequality, exclusion, segregation and socio-spatial injustice (Tyler & Skeggs, 2026).

What emerges is a city-dweller who is not, in any substantive sense, a citizen, but rather an
ethos shaped through relations of power, domination and subjection, in relation to which
they resist, submit, or accommodate themselves—thereby unsettling the modern
assumption that citizenship is an almost inalienable attribute.
The irruption of land mafias produces, among other effects, a radical shift towards the
spatialisation of urban illegality in direct competition with state planning dynamics across
various institutional scales. This has significant implications for the erosion of ordering
processes that are central to the very essence of the political which, as a distinctive act in
the production of citizenship, seeks to erect an order different from that which would be
generated by human misery left to its own devices (Valdecantos, 2016) and by the
imposition of homo economicus as the guiding criterion for social reality—under the
supposed primacy of an economic order positioned hierarchically above all other possible
configurations.
Discussion
As already noted, both before and after urban neoliberalism, struggles for land and
housing for vulnerable groups largely unfolded in explicitly political terms (Herbert &
Brown, 2006). There is broad agreement that the rollout of neoliberal policies in urban
governance marked a turning point from which logics of land commodification and
profound transformations in access to housing emerged. Yet movements, organisations
and narratives persisted that, from a political standpoint, sought solutions to the housing
deficit affecting the most marginalised sectors, in opposition to allocation premised on
market rules (Cardemil Pérez, 2018; Cobos, 2014; Colin et al., 2023; Hidalgo Dattwyler et al.,
2016, 2017; Hidalgo & Janoschka, 2014; Imilan et al., 2016; Rodríguez & Rodríguez, 2009).
In parallel, the literature documents the imposition and consolidation of a neoliberal–
capitalist perspective as the default answer to the housing deficit, which has grown
alarmingly from the pandemic to the present. Policy debates repeatedly invoke the
supposedly transitory nature of the crisis and promote expanded public–private
partnerships and the formal housing market as the appropriate formula for addressing
rising demand.
The pursuit of security and protection of private property through the privatisation of
urban land—while intensifying socio-spatial fragmentation—narrows the scope for
housing movements and obstructs informal, politically driven strategies that seek to
resignify land as more than a consumer good. As the TECHO–CES (2023) study shows,
privatisation has not discouraged loteos brujos or other forms of illegal subdivision.
Privatisation does not impede the operation of land mafias; instead, they shift land’s status
as a tradable asset into an alternative informal market and disregard the legality of state-
and market-regulated property.
26

### Page 27

The operation of land mafias thus produces a neofracture within informal practices aimed
at securing access to urban land and housing. Their dynamics rest on a chain of illegal
actions: the organisation of armed groups that occupy public or private land; the allocation
of plots, in exchange for prior payments, to vulnerable populations—often migrants; and
the protection of occupations against eviction, local resistance and state intervention. The
state frequently fails to deploy its repressive powers, which could in principle combat
illegality and protect legitimate property but whose effectiveness depends on
implementation and respect for due process. Certain layers of institutional–state power
sympathise with rhetoric framing occupations as legitimate routes to dignified housing and
downplay the rights of affected owners, including the state itself when public assets are
taken. In some cases, authorities even acquire occupied land to allocate it to illegal settlers,
with outcomes ranging from disproportionate benefits for occupants to losses for original
owners.
The boundary between the moral and the illegal dimensions of land occupations is
contested in the literature. While some defend seizures as instruments of spatial justice,
the criminal organisation of space through armed and violent practices introduces serious
aggravating factors that sit uneasily with any notion of a just “right to occupy”. Prospects
of financial gain—such as the expectation that a public authority will later purchase
occupied land—may even lead landowners to encourage illegal occupations. Yet available
data do not allow us to gauge how widespread such arrangements are in Chilean informal
settlements, highlighting the need for larger-scale research.
Autonomous law also comes into play: an inflexible approach that seeks to safeguard land
ownership through strict application of legal norms, with little regard for social or ethical
nuances (Rongiyati, 2019). In such a framework, property rights are robustly protected
while the claims of non-owners are delegitimised, collapsing social justice into procedural
justice. Where this orientation prevails, land mafias may escalate conflict and violence and
strategically overlap with other struggles for land that enjoy more favourable public
opinion and backing from segments of socio-political elites. Moreover, state inflexibility
fuels corruption and the sophistication of illegality, at times rendering complaints, lawsuits
or the enforcement of criminal and procedural law practically unworkable (Rongiyati,
2019).
Within this landscape, public officials and security actors occupy an ambivalent position
that further feeds the neofracture. Field testimonies point to a combination of bureaucratic
indifference—reflected in the normalisation of everyday violence in informal settlements—
isolated episodes of active collusion between local authorities and illegal operators, and
tacit tolerance by police and prosecutors, who prioritise other crimes or desist in the face
of evidentiary difficulties. Without imputing specific criminal responsibilities, these
indications suggest a state that is not only unable to intervene effectively, but at times
becomes enmeshed in the very circuits of extraction, reinforcing the asymmetry between
the vulnerability of residents and the relative impunity of those who control the land
business. It is worth specifying what state action looks like when it does occur. The
27

### Page 28

dominant repertoire is securitised and episodic: large eviction operations concentrated on
the most visible occupations, punctual police raids following media attention, and the
deployment of exceptional powers in contexts of heightened public concern over
migration and crime. These interventions target the settlement — and therefore the
residents — rather than the organisational structure of the mafia, whose leadership, capital
and document-forging capacity typically survive the eviction and relocate the operation
elsewhere. Our material yields no evidence of a systematic, structural symbiosis between
state institutions and land mafias; what it documents instead is an asymmetric pattern in
which episodic repression of residents coexists with prolonged neglect of organisers,
occasionally punctuated by localised collusion. Paradoxically, the securitised framing itself
benefits the criminal enterprise: by casting mafia-controlled settlements as security
problems rather than housing problems, it deters housing agencies, municipalities and
civil-society organisations from engaging with residents, thereby deepening the captivity
on which the mafias’ actuarial logic depends.
On state inaction: "We have filed complaints with the municipality, the police, the
prosecutor's office. Everyone says it's not their jurisdiction, or they need more evidence, or
they are waiting for a court order. Meanwhile, the mafia keeps selling plots" (Municipal
housing officer, anonymised, June 2024, medium-sized city, northern Chile).
From a political perspective, logics of responsive law (Rongiyati, 2019)—closer to
discourses on the right to the city—offer a different orientation. Here, the aim is to balance
the demand for housing solutions with the promotion of justice by considering both
property rights and the wellbeing associated with access to homeownership. Public and
citizen participation is placed at the centre of decisions over urban land use, seeking to
minimise harm to owners while curbing the influence of land mafias as shadow
intermediaries. Equity and fair compensation, within a framework of legality and respect for
property rights, become guiding principles.
A comparative view of land mafias across Latin America reveals several conditions that
facilitate their operation: state inaction, which allows the phenomenon to proliferate; legal
gaps in land policy, often linked to this immobility; the mafias’ modus operandi as recent,
highly organised actors that openly challenge state and institutional authority; and the
acquiescence or direct involvement of state officials—a factor that may be inversely related
to efforts to eradicate such practices.
This constellation of practices forces a rethinking of campamentización that explicitly
incorporates criminality, illegality, conflict and urban violence. Land mafias provide short-
term housing solutions through strategies that challenge the role of the state and both
broad and narrow understandings of public and private land ownership, in rural and urban
settings alike. The dynamics described point to epistemic, distributive and
intergenerational injustices that affect not only present habitability but also the future
sustainability of urban space. They may deepen the neofracture, or else prompt the
adoption—within Quadrant II of Figure 1—of re-politicising strategies centred on urban
land and access to housing.

### Page 29

The experience of the Los Arenales macro-settlement in Antofagasta offers a powerful
counterpoint to mafia-dominated scenarios. As shown by previous research, dense
community organisation, the construction of cartographic and political participation
devices, and sustained engagement with the municipality and housing agencies have
enabled progress in regularisation and the provision of basic infrastructure without the
intermediation of criminal land enterprises on the urban fringe of a regional capital
(Vergara-Perucich & Arias-Loyola, 2025; Vergara-Perucich, 2021; Vergara-Perucich &
Barramuño, 2023). Los Arenales is not a “vacant” space awaiting mafia capture; it emerges
as a hybrid formation between Quadrants II and IV, where informal occupation is anchored
in neighbourhood self-government and co-production of public policy that limits the entry
of violent actors. Such protective cases suggest that neofracture is not an inevitable destiny
of campamentización, but a contingent outcome of specific configurations linking
community, state and territorial property markets.
In sum, an adequate conceptualisation of the assemblages associated with land mafias
must recognise that residents’ practices become layered with new forms of illegality, while
their motivations and leaders are cast under a cloud of corruption. This unfolds in a context
where many Latin American cities exhibit stark dualities in the distribution of wealth and
poverty and in the coexistence of formal and informal habitats, each with distinct
temporalities and intensities. Against this backdrop, the article posits the emergence of a
neofracture within Quadrant II of Figure 1, separating earlier forms of struggle and
acquisition of land for informal housing from contemporary arrangements dominated by
mafias as an alternative, hybrid form of urban violence that exploits the housing deficit and
precariousness, extracting value from the material needs of the city’s marginalised
populations.
Conclusions
In normative terms, the argument developed in this article shows that land mafias do more
than reorganise illegal circuits of access to land: they reshape the moral economy of
informality by transforming campamentización into a mechanism for extracting rent from
already dispossessed households. In doing so, they deepen the stigmatisation of migrants
and precarious residents while embedding illicit markets in the very dynamics of urban
expansion. Precisely for this reason, it is crucial to distinguish sharply between those who
are compelled to purchase plots as a last resort for securing shelter and those who design,
protect and profit from these criminalised arrangements. A more fully elaborated ethical
lens prevents the notion of urban neofracture from sliding into punitive narratives about
informal settlements and, instead, uses it to denaturalise the regimes of economic coercion
through which property, security and market logics are imposed on popular sectors.
The findings also have clear implications for public action, understood not as technocratic
fine-tuning but as a project to dismantle these coercive regimes. Monitoring and targeted
prosecution should focus on organisers of illegal subdivisions rather than on residents;
29

### Page 30

anti-corruption safeguards must disrupt the institutional alliances that allow mafias to
operate with impunity; emergency housing programmes and public land banks can
undercut captive demand for criminally mediated plots; regularisation pathways should
protect households without rewarding intermediaries; and participatory planning
instruments can recentre the social function of land over its strictly financial valorisation.
Taken together, these measures point towards a re-politicisation of property and urban
planning in which informal residents are treated as rights-bearing subjects rather than as
security problems or mere consumers of space.
Finally, placing the Chilean case in dialogue with analogous configurations in Colombia
(tierreros), Peru (traficantes de terrenos), Mexico (paracaidistas, lotificadores) and Brazil
(milícias, grilagem) suggests that urban neofracture captures a broader mutation in the
relations between informality, violence and urban production across Latin America (Ávila
Martínez & Carmona Rojas, 2021; Botero Ospina, 2022; Connolly, 2009; Müller, 2024;
Calderón, 2016). These circuits of criminalised land allocation operate as urban frontiers of
extractivism, where value is mined from the basic need for shelter and articulated to wider
processes of planetary urbanisation. Treating neofracture as a comparative category thus
opens an agenda for examining how different constellations of state power, property
regimes and illicit markets shape the uneven geographies of housing, dispossession and
resistance in the contemporary urban world.
